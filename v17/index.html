The Worst Analysis Textbooks

Honest reviews of dishonest pedagogy

Measure Twice, Prove Never

by Leibniz Vandermonde & Carl Fibonachos

The authors promise a “gentle introduction to measure theory” in the preface, then open Chapter 1 with “Let μ be a signed Radon measure on a locally compact Hausdorff space.” By page four I had switched majors. By page twelve my advisor had switched majors. The only gentle thing about this book is how quietly it destroys your will to live.

Epsilon of Faith: A Spiritual Journey Through Limits

by Augusta Ada Borelace

Borelace treats every epsilon-delta proof like a Zen koan, insisting the reader “feel the convergence within.” There is a two-page meditation exercise between Theorems 3.4 and 3.5. The index includes entries for “enlightenment, mathematical” and “suffering, noble eightfold path of.” I have never been less enlightened. My delta remains unspecified.

Real Analysis in the Key of B-Flat

by Jean-Pierre Serge Fourier-Croissant

Every theorem is presented as a musical composition. Chapter 7 is titled “Sonata for Compact Sets and Broken Dreams.” The Heine-Borel theorem is recast as a fugue in four voices, none of which prove anything. There are dynamic markings in the margins—fortissimo next to important lemmas, pianissimo next to the ones the author couldn’t actually prove. I read the whole thing and still can’t tell if it’s analysis or performance art.

Topology for the Friendless

by Hausdorff McMuffin III

McMuffin opens with the dedication “To my students, who will never read this, and my colleagues, who wish they hadn’t.” The problems are labeled Easy, Medium, Hard, and Unknowable. Chapter 9 is just a blank page with a footnote reading “The proof is beneath you.” Strangely, this is the most honest textbook I own.

Banach Spaces and Where to Find Them

by Emmeline Noether-Clue & Ramanujan von Struggle

The title is misleading. You will not find the Banach spaces. They are introduced in Chapter 3, declared “trivially obvious” in Chapter 5, and never mentioned again. Meanwhile, 200 pages are devoted to a personal feud between the co-authors conducted entirely through passive-aggressive footnotes. Footnote 47 simply reads: “Ramanujan von Struggle is incorrect, as usual.”

The Unbearable Completeness of Being: Cauchy Sequences and You

by Dirichlet O’Lebesgue

O’Lebesgue has written what can only be described as an existential crisis in textbook form. Every chapter begins with an epigraph from Kafka. The definition of a Cauchy sequence spans three pages and includes the phrase “but what does it mean to be close, truly?” Exercise 1.1 asks the reader to “prove that meaning converges.” It does not. Neither does this book.

A collection of texts that have caused more career changes than the 2008 financial crisis.
