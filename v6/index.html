A Prestigious Collection of Texts That Have Shattered More Dreams Than All Other Academic Disciplines Combined

The Gilded Collection · Volume VI

The Hall of Infamy

What you are about to witness is not a reading list. It is a monument to pedagogical hubris—a velvet-lined reliquary of the most exquisitely dreadful analysis textbooks ever committed to paper. Each volume below has been inducted into our permanent collection after rigorous evaluation by a panel of traumatized graduate students, embittered adjuncts, and at least one tenured professor who wept openly during deliberations. Browse at your own peril. We accept no liability for existential crises, career changes, or sudden urges to attend law school.

I

Convergence and Consequence: A First Course in Regret Theory

by Bernhard Reemann & Niels Henrick Drivel

0.5 / 5

“Reemann and Drivel have achieved something I once thought impossible: they have written a textbook that is simultaneously too rigorous and too vague. Every theorem is stated with suffocating formality, then proven by gesturing at a lemma from Chapter 19—a chapter which does not exist. The index references itself. I have been in therapy for three semesters.”

— Dr. Constance Halliwell, Dept. of Applied Regret, University of Edinburgh
II

Sigma-Algebras and the Void: Measure Theory for the Already Broken

by Giuseppe Peaknow

1.0 / 5

“The AUDACITY of Chapter 7. I literally had to put this book down, pour myself a glass of rosé, and stare at the ceiling for twenty minutes. The proof of Fubini’s theorem is four pages long and every single line says ‘by a similar argument.’ SIMILAR TO WHAT, GIUSEPPE? I am but one man. I have a finite number of tears. This book tested that upper bound and found it wanting. Absolutely unhinged proof structure. I need a moment.”

— Tristan Bellvue, third-year PhD candidate & survivor
III

The Banach–Tarskin Paradox and Other Reasons to Drop Out

by Augustin-Louis Crunchy & Sofia Kovalevskya-Adjacent

1.5 / 5

“Crunchy and Kovalevskya-Adjacent open with the phrase ‘We assume the reader is comfortable with the basics.’ What follows is 600 pages that are comfortable with nothing. The exercises range from trivial to Fields Medal. There is no middle ground. Problem 3.14 simply reads: ‘Prove or disprove.’ That’s it. No statement. Just an imperative and a threat.”

— Prof. Yuki Tanaka, Kyoto Institute of Mathematical Disillusionment
IV

Pointwise Convergence of My Will to Live: Sequences, Series, and Sorrow

by Karl Weaselstrass & Pierre-Simon Lacross

0.5 / 5

“Every diagram in this book is hand-drawn, poorly scanned, and rotated eleven degrees counterclockwise. The notation changes between chapters without warning—what is ‘ε’ in Chapter 2 becomes ‘η’ in Chapter 3 and ‘the reader’s burden’ in Chapter 5. The bibliography cites two papers, both by the authors, both unpublished, both allegedly ‘forthcoming’ since 1987.”

— Dr. Eleanora Voss, Society for the Preservation of Mathematical Sanity
V

Functional Analysis Without the Function or the Analysis

by Henri Lemon-besgue & Andrei Kold-mogorov

1.0 / 5

“Oh, this book. THIS book. I bought it because my advisor said it was ‘the standard reference.’ Standard for WHOM? Cryptographers? Sadists? Page one assumes you have internalized all of Rudin, Folland, AND some secret third text that apparently only exists in the authors’ shared fever dream. I highlighted every confusing passage and now the entire book is yellow. Every. Single. Page. I’m not angry, I’m disappointed. Actually no—I am furious.”

— Marcus Chen-Delacroix, who switched to data science and has never looked back
