For every bad textbook, there exists a worse one.

A semi-regular chronicle of the most baffling, infuriating, and occasionally hilarious real analysis textbooks I've encountered across fifteen years of grad school, office clear-outs, and ill-advised used-bookstore purchases. Names may be real. Pain is always real.

March 28, 2026

Lebesgue Integration and the Limits of Human Endurance

by Prof. Henri Lemon-besgue · Springer-Verlost, 2018

Rating: 0.5 / 5

I want to be very clear about something. I am not a weak person. I have run marathons. I have given birth without an epidural. I once ate gas station sushi on a dare and went to work the next day. But nothing — NOTHING — could have prepared me for Chapter 7 of this textbook.

It started innocently. "Let us now consider the Lebesgue integral," the author wrote, as if suggesting we consider a nice walk in the park. I settled into my chair with coffee and a false sense of security. By page 312, I was on the floor of my apartment in the fetal position, weeping into a pile of scratch paper. By page 318, I had called my mother to tell her I loved her. By page 324 — the section on absolutely continuous functions — I experienced what I can only describe as a complete evacuation of everything I once believed about mathematics.

My roommate found me at 4 AM staring at the wall, muttering "almost everywhere… almost everywhere…" She called an ambulance. The paramedic, a former math major, took one look at the book and just shook his head. "Lebesgue?" he asked. I could only nod.

Do not read this book. Do not look at this book. If someone offers you this book, run. Run and do not look back.

March 10, 2026

A Gentle Introduction to Real Analysis

by Prof. Augustin-Louis Cau-She-Didn't · Dover Delusions, 2020

Rating: 1 / 5

Finally, a real analysis textbook for people like me! For years I've struggled with regular analysis textbooks, what with their intimidating blackness of ink and their aggressive, masculine theorems. But this "Gentle Introduction" has changed everything. The epsilons are printed in a slightly smaller font, which I find much less threatening. The deltas come in a softer shade, as though to say, "Don't worry. I won't hurt you." The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem is introduced with a content warning.

Before this book, I couldn't even hold a proof. I'd try to write "suppose for contradiction" and my hand would simply give out. My advisor suggested I try proving things "gently," perhaps by assuming the hypothesis AND the conclusion simultaneously. This book validates that approach.

I do wish the book came with a set of training wheels for the Heine-Borel theorem, but I suppose we can't have everything. One must learn to wobble through compactness on one's own eventually.

I showed this book to my colleague who uses Rudin. He laughed. I showed him my teaching evaluations. He stopped laughing.

February 14, 2026

Principles of Mathematical Analysis

by Prof. Walter Rude-in · McGraw-Hill, 1976

Rating: 5 / 5

I am a professor. I have been teaching for twenty-two years. In that time, I have encountered one student who made me question the foundations of mathematics education in this country. I will call him Kevin.

Kevin submitted a proof that all real numbers are rational. When I pointed out the error, he submitted a revised proof that all rational numbers are integers. When I explained why this was wrong, he submitted a third proof that integers don't exist. It was the most logically consistent of the three.

Kevin once asked me, in front of the entire lecture hall, "If epsilon is so small, why don't we just use zero?" I have thought about this question every single day since. I will think about it until I die.

Kevin turned in the midterm blank except for the sentence "I am not convinced that the real numbers are real" written in large letters across the front page. He received a 4 out of 100 because I gave him points for philosophical bravery.

Kevin passed the course with a D-minus. He is now a quantitative analyst at a hedge fund and makes four times my salary.

This textbook is very clear and well-written and I used it to teach Kevin and it didn't matter. Five stars.

January 20, 2026

Introduction to Topological Sadness

by Prof. Karl Why-erstrass · Academic Regress, 2015

Rating: 1 / 5

TIFU by buying this textbook.

So this actually happened three years ago but I'm only now emotionally ready to talk about it. I was a sophomore, double-majoring in math and what I now realize was poor life decisions. My advisor said topology would be "fun." My advisor is no longer my advisor.

Week 1: "A topology on X is a collection of subsets…" Cool. Fine. I can do this.

Week 3: I learn that a coffee mug and a donut are the same thing. I stare at my coffee mug for forty-five minutes. My roommate asks if I'm okay. I am not okay, but I tell him I am.

Week 5: I dream about open sets. In the dream, I am an open set. I contain none of my boundary points. I wake up crying.

Week 8: I try to explain to my girlfriend what a "connected space" is. She says she thinks we should see other people. I want to argue but I cannot even prove our relationship is path-connected.

Week 11: The professor introduces quotient spaces. I go to office hours. The professor looks at me with what I can only describe as pity. "It gets worse," he says. He is not wrong.

Week 14: I get a C+. My GPA is in shambles. My girlfriend is dating a business major. I have not slept in what I believe is nine days. I know what a manifold is. I do not know what happiness is.

Anyway, the textbook is well-organized and the exercises are reasonable. 1 star.

December 5, 2025

Principles of Mathematical Analysis

by W. Rudin · McGraw-Hill, 1976

Rating: 5 / 5

I was a first-generation college student. Nobody in my family had gone past high school. When I got to university, I didn't know what office hours were. I didn't know you could ask professors questions. I sat in the back of every class and I didn't talk to anyone.

I was failing real analysis. Not the dignified kind of failing where you understand the material but make errors. The kind where you stare at the page and the symbols might as well be Sumerian. I was going to drop out. I had already looked up bus tickets home.

There was a guy in my class — older student, maybe 30, always sat in the front. I'd never spoken to him. One night I was in the library at 2 AM, staring at chapter 2 of Rudin, and he sat down across from me. He didn't ask if I wanted help. He just opened his copy to the same page and started explaining. Metric spaces. Open sets. Compactness. He explained it like he had all the time in the world. We were there until the library closed.

He did this every week for the rest of the semester. I passed with a B. It was the best grade I'd ever gotten in anything. On the last day of class I tried to thank him and I started crying, which was embarrassing. He just said, "Someone did this for me once. Do it for someone else."

I'm a math professor now. I hold extra office hours on Thursday nights for students who look like they're about to give up. I keep a copy of Baby Rudin on my desk. It's the same copy. The one that guy helped me through. The binding is destroyed. The margins are full of his handwriting and mine.

Five stars. Not for the book. Rudin is a terrible pedagogical writer and I will die on that hill. Five stars for what happened around the book. Five stars for the theorem that says: someone helped you, now you help someone. No proof required.
