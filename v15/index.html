The Worst Analysis Textbooks Ever Written

Dispatches from the bleak interior of a graduate student's bookshelf — where proofs go to die and hope is left as an exercise.

March 12, 2026 · Reviewed by S. Gauthier

"Measure Theory Without Mercy"

by Prof. Henri Lebesgross — Éditions du Néant, 2019

★ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆

I purchased this textbook at the start of my second year, lured by the back-cover promise that it would "gently guide the reader through the landscape of measure and integration." Gentle is not the word I would choose. By page eleven, Lebesgross has constructed a non-measurable set, defined the outer measure in terms of an infimum over coverings he has not yet introduced, and informed the reader that "intuition is the enemy of rigour." The exercises at the end of Chapter 1 include proving the Banach-Tarski paradox "from first principles," with the cheerful hint: "Recall the Axiom of Choice." I did not, in fact, recall the Axiom of Choice. I recalled nothing. I was on page eleven.

"The student who requires motivation should consult a therapist, not a textbook." — from the Preface

What truly distinguishes this book is its commitment to misery as a pedagogical tool. Definitions are buried mid-proof, labeled only by the section number in which they first appear — never by name. Want to look up "sigma-algebra"? Good luck: it is introduced inside the proof of Proposition 2.3.7(b), referred to thereafter simply as "the structure from 2.3.7(b)." Theorems are stated in their most general form first, with the comprehensible special case relegated to a remark six pages later, phrased as "the reader will have no difficulty verifying." I had every difficulty. I verified nothing.

And yet, I cannot deny that the final chapter on Radon-Nikodym derivatives is almost beautiful — a crystalline five pages that made me briefly forget the preceding four hundred pages of suffering. Perhaps that is Lebesgross's true thesis: that enlightenment requires despair as a prerequisite. In that case, this book is the most effective text ever written.

— S. Gauthier, Université de Montréal

February 28, 2026 · Reviewed by Dmitri K.

"Functional Analysis for the Friendless"

by Prof. Stéfan Banachronism — Springer-Verluft, 2021

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Let me be absolutely clear: this book ruined my winter. I had plans. I had a social life, or at least the faint silhouette of one. Then my advisor placed "Functional Analysis for the Friendless" on my desk with a look that I can only describe as clinical indifference, and said, "You'll want to get through chapters one through nine by January." Chapters one through nine span 540 pages. January was three weeks away. Reader, I did not get through chapters one through nine by January. I got through chapter two and a nervous condition.

"We omit the proof, which is long but not difficult. The determined reader will find it occupies a rewarding afternoon." — Chapter 4, on a proof that took me eleven days

Banachronism writes with the serene detachment of a man who has never once struggled with a concept in his life and therefore cannot fathom why you might. Every chapter opens with a quote from a dead mathematician, as though Riesz himself is looking down from the afterlife to remind you that you are not, and will never be, a Riesz. The exercises range from "verify this trivial identity" (not trivial) to "generalize Theorem 7.4 to arbitrary locally convex topological vector spaces" (I don't even have the vocabulary to describe how un-generalizable that felt at 2 a.m.). The index is four pages long. For a 700-page book. Four pages.

Oh, and the binding? Fell apart by week two. Pages 211 through 246 now live loose in a Ziploc bag. Which is fitting, because my sense of mathematical identity also came unbound somewhere around page 211. Banachronism would probably call that "a homeomorphism between the text and the reader's psyche." Two stars, and I'm being generous because the Hahn-Banach section was genuinely well-written, even if it cost me my will to live.

— Dmitri K., ETH Zürich (surviving, barely)

January 15, 2026 · Reviewed by Jayden St. Hilbert

"Sequences, Series, and Emotional Damage"

by Prof. Augustin-Louis Cauchemar — Academic Void Press, 2022

★ ☆ ☆ ☆ ☆

Okay so I need everyone to understand something: I am a SMART person. I got into this program. I passed qualifying exams. I have been told, by credible adults, that I have "mathematical promise." And then Cauchemar's book looked me dead in my face on page thirty-seven and said "it is obvious that the subsequence converges" and honey, it was NOT obvious. Nothing about this book is obvious. The only thing obvious is that Augustin-Louis Cauchemar wakes up every morning and chooses violence against students who were just trying to learn about pointwise convergence in PEACE.

"The proof is left as an exercise. Indeed, most of this chapter is left as an exercise." — Chapter 6 (the entire chapter is nine exercises and one definition)

Chapter 6 is, and I cannot stress this enough, UNHINGED. It is titled "Uniform Convergence and Its Consequences" but contains almost no actual text. There is one definition at the top — just the definition of uniform convergence — and then nine exercises that collectively ask you to independently reconstruct the entire theory. Exercise 6.4 says "Prove the Weierstrass M-test" with no hints. Exercise 6.7 says "Determine all conditions under which term-by-term integration is valid" as though that is a CASUAL thing to ask someone. I sat in the library for six hours staring at Exercise 6.7 and the only thing I determined was that I should have gone to law school.

The real kicker? The book has no solutions manual. I emailed the publisher. I emailed Cauchemar himself. The publisher said the solutions manual "exists only in the author's mind." Cauchemar replied — and I quote — "The journey is the solution." Sir, the JOURNEY has given me an eye twitch that activates every time someone says the word "epsilon." One star. One single, trembling, traumatized star. I'm going to go lie down now.

— Jayden St. Hilbert, NYU (sending this from the campus counseling center)
