Measure Theory Without Motivation: A Joyless Expedition

by Hernando Lebesgué

2019 · 814 pages · Arid Academic Press
★ ★ ★ ★ ★ 2 / 5

Lebesgué opens by stating that the Lebesgue integral "needs no introduction," then proceeds to not introduce it for 300 pages. The first three chapters are pure sigma-algebra manipulation with no geometric intuition whatsoever. I kept waiting for a diagram, a picture, even a vaguely suggestive squiggle in the margin. Nothing. This book treats visual learners like a myth.

The exercises range from trivial to unsolved research problems with no indication of which is which. Problem 4.17 asks you to "verify" a result that took Borel two decades to prove. I verified that I wanted to drop out instead. Two stars because the binding is genuinely excellent and it makes a decent monitor stand.

Sequences, Series, and Suffering: A First Course in Convergence

by Augustin-Louis Cauché

2021 · 522 pages · Vanishing Gradient Books
★ ★ ★ ★ ★ 1 / 5

I am going to be so vulnerable right now. I bought this book because the cover was a tasteful mauve and I thought, "maybe this is the one that finally makes Cauchy sequences click." Reader. READER. This book made me understand Cauchy sequences less than before I opened it. I have gone backwards. My knowledge is diverging. I am a living counterexample to the claim that studying improves understanding.

Chapter 7 is titled "Uniform Convergence: It's Not That Hard" and then proceeds to be EXACTLY that hard, with zero remorse. Cauché writes like someone who has never once had to explain anything to another human being and honestly? Iconic behavior but NOT in a textbook. I threw this across my apartment and it knocked over my diffuser. My room smelled like eucalyptus and failure for a week. One star. Absolutely unhinged.

Topological Diversions for the Analytically Inclined

by Bernadette Riemannova

2017 · 396 pages · Nowhere Dense Publishing
★ ★ ★ ★ ★ 3 / 5

Honestly this one is almost good and that makes it worse. Riemannova clearly understands the material deeply and has moments of genuine clarity — the section on compactness in Chapter 5 is the best explanation I have encountered anywhere. But then she follows it immediately with 40 pages of category theory "prerequisites" that were not, in fact, prerequisite to anything in the remaining chapters.

The real crime is the index, which appears to have been generated by a random process. Looking up "open set" sends you to a page about the author's sabbatical in Göttingen. Three stars because when this book is good, it's genuinely very good, and also because I'm grading on the curve that is my life now.

Functional Analysis for People Who Peaked in Linear Algebra

by Stefan Banacheck

2023 · 670 pages · Unbounded Operator Press
★ ★ ★ ★ ★ 2 / 5

The title is a personal attack and I respect it. Unfortunately, that is the last respectful thing Banacheck does. Page one assumes familiarity with "basic" Hilbert space theory, which — sure — but by page twelve we're in the spectral theorem with no warning, no warmup, no gentle hand on the shoulder saying "this will be okay." The pacing of this book suggests the author wrote it during a single manic weekend.

Most damning: every single proof ends with "the remaining details are left to the reader," including the proof of the main theorem the entire book is building toward. Banacheck essentially wrote 670 pages of preamble and then said "now you do it." I have never felt more gaslit by a piece of bound paper. Two stars because the chapter on dual spaces was admittedly well-organized, even if it did make me cry.
