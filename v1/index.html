The Worst Analysis Textbooks

Because suffering builds character, and these books build a lot of it.

Welcome to the definitive collection of analysis textbooks that have driven generations of mathematics students to reconsider their life choices. Each title below has been carefully selected for its unique ability to transform optimistic undergraduates into hollow-eyed graduate students who flinch at the phrase "left as an exercise for the reader." We review them so you don't have to read them. You will anyway.

Principles of Real Analysis: A Rigorous Approach to Confusion

by Dr. Heinrich Klemmer

"Klemmer writes with the warmth of a Siberian winter and the clarity of a fog bank. Chapter 3 alone contains 47 theorems, each proven by referencing a different theorem from a different chapter, creating a closed loop of despair that topologists would admire."

— Prof. Lydia Ashworth, The Mathematical Pessimist Quarterly

Measure Theory and Integration: Why God Abandoned Us

by R. Planck-Whitfield & S. Okafor

"The authors promise an 'intuitive' introduction to Lebesgue measure on page one. By page two, they have introduced seven Greek letters and a sigma-algebra. The exercises range in difficulty from 'impossible' to 'the authors are clearly working through a personal vendetta against the reader.'"

— Marcus Tran, Notices of the Disillusioned

Functional Analysis for the Brave and the Desperate

by Prof. Valentina Kozlova

"Kozlova's textbook is 800 pages long and contains exactly one example. It appears on page 12 and is the identity function. The remaining 788 pages are an unbroken wilderness of abstraction that makes Bourbaki look like a children's picture book."

— Dr. James Oduya, Journal of Regrettable Academic Purchases

An Introduction to Complex Analysis (That Is Neither)

by G. Neville St. Clair

"The title promises 'An Introduction,' which is technically true in the same way that being thrown into the ocean is an introduction to swimming. The index entry for 'trivial' redirects to 'see: every proof in this book.' I have never felt less intelligent than when reading Chapter 9, and I have a PhD."

— Dr. Priya Chandrasekaran, Annals of Barely Passing
