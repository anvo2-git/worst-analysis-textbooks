The Epsilon Errata

For every bad textbook, there exists a worse one.

A semi-regular chronicle of the most baffling, infuriating, and occasionally hilarious real analysis textbooks I've encountered across fifteen years of grad school, office clear-outs, and ill-advised used-bookstore purchases. Names may be real. Pain is always real.

March 22, 2026

A Compact Introduction to Measure Spaces

by Henri LeBaguette · Springer-Verlost, 2019

Rating: 1.5 / 5

LeBaguette opens the preface by claiming this text is "suitable for any student with a passing familiarity with the real line." This is technically true in the same way that the Atlantic Ocean is suitable for anyone with a passing familiarity with water. By page forty, we are constructing outer measures on abstract semi-rings without so much as a motivating example. The index entry for "intuition" redirects to a blank page, which I'm choosing to believe is intentional performance art.

What stings most is that LeBaguette clearly knows the material deeply. You can feel a brilliant lecture course buried somewhere under the notation. Entire paragraphs read like hastily transcribed chalkboard ramblings — the kind where the professor keeps saying "this is obvious" while furiously erasing something that was, apparently, not obvious at all. The exercises range from trivial to genuinely unsolved, with no indication of which is which. A frustrating book from a clearly gifted mathematician who simply does not believe communication is part of mathematics.

February 8, 2026

Sequences, Series, and Emotional Damage

by Bernhard Riemannaise · Dover Regrets, 2014

Rating: 0.5 / 5

Oh, where do I even BEGIN. I bought this book because the Dover edition was six dollars and I thought, "How bad can it be?" Reader, it can be so bad. The first chapter is titled "Preliminary Notions" and it is ninety-three pages long. NINETY-THREE. That is not a preliminary notion, that is a lifestyle. I had to take a personal day after Chapter 2 introduced seventeen different epsilon-delta definitions and then casually informed me that "the motivated reader will find the connections self-evident." I am a motivated reader, Bernhard! I am HIGHLY motivated! Specifically, I am motivated to throw this book into the sea!

The real crime is the exercises. Every single one says "Prove or disprove" with no hints, no answers, and a little smiley face icon next to the ones Riemannaise considers "straightforward." I completed exactly zero smiley-face problems. Not one. I showed Problem 4.17 to my advisor and she laughed for forty-five seconds and then said "Oh, you're serious." This textbook is a menace to public health and I am keeping my copy only because it is the exact right height to level my wobbly desk. One half star for structural integrity.

January 15, 2026

Functional Analysis for the Working Mathematician

by Grothendiaper & Bananach · Cambridge Unitress Press, 2021

Rating: 2.5 / 5

This is the most aggressively mediocre textbook I have ever read, and I mean that almost as a compliment. Grothendiaper and Bananach have produced something that is never terrible enough to be funny and never good enough to be useful. It occupies a kind of uncanny valley of mathematical exposition where every definition is technically correct, every proof is technically complete, and every page makes you feel like you're reading a legal document about Banach spaces. The Hahn-Banach theorem gets a full fourteen-page treatment that left me understanding it less than when I started.

On the positive side, the typesetting is genuinely beautiful — someone at Cambridge clearly spent real time on the LaTeX, and the diagrams of operator spectra are some of the best I've seen in print. There's also a surprisingly useful appendix on the history of the discipline that made me briefly forget I was reading a textbook at all. If only that energy had been applied to, say, explaining what a dual space is before using it in seventeen consecutive theorems. A handsome book that teaches you nothing, like a well-dressed stranger who refuses to give directions.

December 3, 2025

Topology of the Real Line: A Rigorous Approach

by Augustin-Louis Cauchemar · Elsevier Else-Where, 2017

Rating: 2 / 5

"Rigorous" is doing Herculean labor in this title. Cauchemar's approach to point-set topology is to state every theorem in maximum generality, prove it in a special case, and then assign the general case as an exercise marked "routine." I have now learned that in French-school analysis, "routine" means "requires three lemmas you haven't proven yet and a change of perspective your professor acquired during a sabbatical in 1987." Chapter 5, on connectedness, opens with the sentence "We now abandon all metric assumptions" and proceeds as though this is a gift rather than a threat.

In fairness, there are flashes of real insight. Cauchemar's treatment of compactness via nets is genuinely original, and Theorem 7.14 gave me one of those rare moments where a difficult concept suddenly clicks into place. But these moments are buried under pages of unmotivated abstraction and a notation system that uses both subscripts and superscripts simultaneously on the same variable, sometimes with different meanings depending on context. I ended up creating a twelve-page personal glossary just to read Chapter 3. A book that rewards perseverance, but perhaps should not require quite so much of it.
